Editor's Note 編 者 按

This issue began with a sentence I did not write. A reader, in a letter we received last winter, asked: "Why do I feel guilty when I rest?" I read the letter four times. I made tea. I read it again. Around it, slowly, an issue began to form.

The Chinese tradition has a long argument with this question. Not a sermon — an argument. Laozi said the empty space inside the bowl is what makes the bowl useful. Zhuangzi said the cook who never sharpens his blade is the cook who has stopped fighting the meat. We have, in eight pieces, tried to listen to that argument plainly, in English, without dressing it up as anything other than what it is — a careful, patient idea about effort.

You will not find advice here. You will find ways of seeing. That is, I think, what an old book is for.

Letter No. 047 Selected · 2026.04.12
I rest. But I still feel guilty. Why?
— S. R. · Brooklyn, NY
Editor's Reply

Guilt around rest is, almost always, guilt around being unobserved. Laozi suggests, in chapter 22, that what is not seen is not less. The bowl is useful because of its emptiness — but the bowl does not feel guilty for being empty. The guilt belongs to us, who learned to value being filled.

Letter No. 044 Selected · 2026.04.08
Why does my room get heavy when my mind does?
— A. M. · Toronto
Editor's Reply

A room is the body's second skin. Zhuangzi tells the story of a butcher whose blade never dulls because he reads the meat — your room reads you back. When the mind is heavy, the room mirrors it before you have the words for it. Sometimes the room is the words.

Letter No. 041 Selected · 2026.04.02
Should I keep trying, or should I let it go?
— K. T. · Singapore
Editor's Reply

Old Chinese thinking refuses your either-or. The I Ching says: when the time is not yet, do not force; when it is, do not hesitate. The hard part is not the choice — it is learning to read which time you are in. We will write you a longer answer, soon.

Forthcoming — Issue 003 · Reading the time

Letter No. 039 Selected · 2026.03.28
Why am I most tired on Sundays, when I do nothing?
— J. P. · Mexico City
Editor's Reply

Because you are not, in fact, doing nothing. You are watching yourself do nothing. Zhuangzi calls this the second mind — the one that observes the first. Real rest, in his telling, is when the second mind goes quiet. We have not yet figured out how. We are working on it.
